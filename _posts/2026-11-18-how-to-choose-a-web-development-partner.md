Your website is not a brochure. It is a sales channel, a trust signal, and often the first real interaction a customer has with your brand. Choosing who builds it is therefore a business decision, not just a technical one.

After delivering projects for non-profits, education teams, and travel brands across India, Nepal, and the USA, we have seen where partnerships succeed—and where they quietly go wrong. This guide is the checklist we wish every client had before signing a proposal.

## 1. Start with the outcome, not the feature list

Many briefs begin with "we need a website with five pages and a contact form." That describes a deliverable, not a goal. A strong partner will ask what the site needs to *do*: generate qualified leads, reduce support tickets, sell products, or make information easier to find.

Write down three measurable outcomes before you compare quotes. For example:

- Increase enquiry form submissions by 30% in six months.
- Cut average page load time to under two seconds.
- Let the team update content without calling a developer.

A developer who challenges your brief is not being difficult. They are protecting the result.

## 2. Look for a process you can understand

Ask every candidate to walk you through their workflow from kickoff to launch. You are not looking for fancy terminology. You are listening for clear answers to three questions:

1. **How do we agree on scope?** There should be a written milestone plan with named deliverables.
2. **How do we review progress?** You need a staging link and regular demos, not a surprise reveal weeks later.
3. **What happens when requirements change?** A defined change process is normal; a vague "we'll figure it out" is not.

Our own [four-step process]({{ '/#process' | relative_url }}) exists for exactly this reason: discovery, design, development, and launch each have an outcome you can verify.

## 3. Ask about performance and SEO fundamentals

Design gets the applause; structure gets the traffic. A beautiful site that loads slowly or cannot be crawled will underperform every time.

Ask these questions directly:

- Will the site be responsive by default, and how is mobile tested?
- How are images optimised and served?
- Is semantic HTML used for headings, navigation, and content?
- What happens to existing URLs if this is a redesign?
- Are redirects, metadata, and a sitemap part of launch?

If the answer to any of these is "we'll add SEO later," treat it as a warning sign. Technical SEO is far cheaper to build in from day one than to retrofit after launch. Our guide to [Core Web Vitals]({{ '/blog/core-web-vitals-explained/' | relative_url }}) explains why speed is now a ranking and conversion issue.

## 4. Check communication before you check code

Most project friction comes from silence, not skill. Pay attention to how quickly a prospective partner replies, how clearly they explain trade-offs, and whether they can say no to a bad idea.

A good working relationship has a simple rhythm: a single point of contact, a shared task list, and a scheduled update even when there is nothing dramatic to report. You should never have to chase a status update.

## 5. Ownership matters more than convenience

Ask who owns the domain, hosting, repository, analytics, and content. You want full access to every asset when the engagement ends. A partner who makes ownership complicated is creating dependency, not value.

Before signing, confirm that you will receive:

- Admin access to hosting and the content management system.
- Source code in a repository you control.
- Analytics and Search Console access under your account.
- Documentation for any custom functionality.

## 6. Plan for life after launch

Launch day is the middle of a website's life, not the end. Browsers change, plugins age, and content needs updating. Ask what support looks like: response times, backup frequency, security updates, and the cost of small changes.

A partner who offers [ongoing maintenance]({{ '/#services' | relative_url }}) is signalling that they expect the relationship to continue—and that they have seen what happens when nobody watches a site after go-live.

## Red flags worth walking away from

- No written scope, timeline, or acceptance criteria.
- A portfolio that only shows mockups, never live sites.
- Refusal to share references or previous work.
- "We do everything" with no specialisation or clear process.
- Prices that are dramatically lower than every other quote without an explanation.
- No plan for redirects, analytics, or post-launch support.

## A quick scoring checklist

Rate each partner from one to five on these six points:

| Criterion | What good looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Discovery | Asks about goals, audience, and constraints first |
| Process | Written milestones and regular review opportunities |
| Technical quality | Semantic markup, speed, responsive by default |
| Communication | Clear, prompt, and honest about trade-offs |
| Ownership | You control every account and asset |
| Support | Defined maintenance and response expectations |

The highest score is rarely the most expensive option. It is the team that makes the decisions easiest to understand.
